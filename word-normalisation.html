How the simulation works

Fits. Each word’s trials are pooled across participants and fitted by maximum likelihood with a 4AFC logistic: chance fixed at 25%, and the upper limit either free between 70% and 100% or fixed at 100% (the Gibbins form). With the ceiling fixed, a word that levels off below 100% is fitted with a shallower slope instead, so it tends to be caught by the shallow-slope criterion rather than the low-ceiling one. Cutoffs are fitted in octaves (log₂ Hz), SNR in dB. A word’s midpoint is the centre of its rise from chance to its ceiling (62.5% correct when the ceiling is 100%), and its slope is the slope of that rise at the midpoint, from 0% to 100% of the way up. In %/octave this is the 4AFC slope divided by 0.75; it converts to Gibbins’ and Anderson’s units (100 × k per decade, where k is the Kollmeier slope coefficient) by dividing by log₁₀2 = 0.301. The fit cannot resolve a slope steeper than 833 %/octave (shown as “≥ 833”): such words go from chance to ceiling between two adjacent levels tested.

Target. The target function has the median midpoint and the median slope of the words in the target. Medians are used because several words’ slopes are only known to be very steep, and a median stays correct when fewer than half of the values are bounded like this. By default a word is left out when its ceiling is low (below 86%, levelled off within the levels tested), its midpoint lies outside the levels it was tested at, its slope or midpoint is far outside the others (slope beyond 1.5 interquartile ranges of the log slopes; midpoint beyond 3 interquartile ranges), or it has fewer than 30 trials.

Adjustment. For a requested level N, a word is played at the level where its own rise is as far along as the target’s rise is at N. With the Kollmeier form used by Gibbins this is a straight line on the fitting axis, whatever the number of alternatives: played = midpointword + (sword/starget)(N − midpointtarget), where s is the width of each rise. The basic method sets the ratio to 1, so every word is shifted by the gap between its midpoint and the target’s and keeps its own slope. The O’Beirne/Gibbins method also matches the slope, which makes the adjustment depend on the level: a shallow word gets a larger adjustment the further the request is from the midpoint. The ratio is capped (default ×3) because very steep or very shallow fits would otherwise ask for extreme levels. The ceiling is left out of the match so that every request has an answer; words with a low ceiling are flagged rather than forced. Played cutoffs stay within the filter’s 75 Hz–20 kHz, and every played level stays within the optional limit, which keeps large changes in speech level from becoming a loudness cue in the noise condition.

Runs recorded with normalisation. The test software records both the level the procedure asked for and the level each word was played at. Fits normally use the played level, so these runs add to the word estimates like any other. Choosing Requested levels instead, with only those runs in use, shows how centred the words really were with the table in place; the simulation is switched off then, because it applies to played levels.

The whole test. Its function is the point-by-point average of every word’s fitted function, and its slope is taken at the average’s 62.5% point and expressed in the same rise units as a single word. This does not depend on which words happened to be played at which levels, which the level plans varied from word to word.

Simulation. Every recorded trial is moved to the requested level that would have produced the level it was played at, and each word is then refitted at those requested levels. Applying adjustments to the same data they were estimated from makes every word line up almost by construction, so by default each participant’s trials are adjusted using fits from all the other participants (leave-one-out). That is the fairer preview of how new listeners will do. The word cards and the normalisation table always show the all-participant adjustment, as the test software would use it.

Anderson’s functions. From her thesis (low-pass only; closed set with a 25% floor, open set with a 0% floor), drawn from her SRTs and slopes with the slope read as 100 × k per decade. Her thesis labels these slopes %/octave, but only the per-decade reading reproduces her own average curves. A few closed-set slopes of 1000 %/decade or more are fits that ran to a limit, and are marked as such. Her recordings and listeners differ from these, so the comparison is of shape and spread, not of exact values.
